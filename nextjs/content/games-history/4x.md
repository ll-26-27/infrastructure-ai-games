**The line in one sentence:** history is expansion: the map is empty until someone claims it, and progress is a tree every people climbs in the same order.

## How it went

Space games got there first. *Reach for the Stars* (Strategic Studies Group, 1983) had players colonize star systems and out-build each other, and is called the earliest known commercial example of the genre. [8] But *Civilization* (Sid Meier and Bruce Shelley, MicroProse, September 1991) set the pattern: one settler, a dark map, cities that work the land, a tree of technologies from pottery to spaceflight, and other civilizations to trade with or conquer. Its prototype borrowed from *Empire*, *Railroad Tycoon*, *SimCity* and a 1980 board game named *Civilization*. [1] Two years later a magazine writer previewing the space game *Master of Orion* gave it a rating of "XXXX" for "the essential four X's of any good strategic conquest game": **explore, expand, exploit, exterminate**. The name stuck for the whole genre. [2] [3]

The loop then spread in two directions. Volunteers rebuilt it as free, open code, so anyone can read exactly how it's written: [Freeciv](/gallery/freeciv) (1996) and [Unciv](/gallery/unciv) (2017). [4] [5] And it shrank onto phones: [The Battle of Polytopia](/gallery/polytopia) (2016) fits a game of Civilization's kind of thinking into a bus ride. [6] The same year *Stellaris* (Paradox, 2016) spread the four X's across a whole galaxy. [9] Most people who grew up with phones met the 4X loop in games that don't call themselves 4X at all. [Clash of Clans](/gallery/clash-of-clans) (2012) keeps the building, the upgrade tree and the raids, and turns "exterminate" into attacks on other players' villages. [7] Farming games keep the first three X's and point the fourth at weeds, debris and cave monsters (see [farming and management](/games-history/farming-and-management)).

## What the mechanics argue

- **Land is empty until you claim it.** The map starts dark and the people already living there appear as "barbarians" or "villages" to capture. Critics read this as a colonial story told through rules, whatever the game's text says.
- **Progress has one direction.** The tech tree is the same for everyone, and the only question is how fast you climb it. That is an argument about history.
- **Everything is a resource.** Cities, people and land are worth what they produce. Compare labor allocation.
- **Taking the war out doesn't take out the competition.** Ask where the fourth X went in any game: to rival players, to the clock, or to the land itself. See 4X.

## What critics say

- **Alexander Galloway** on *Civilization*: "To play the game means to play the code of the game." A good player stops reading the history on the screen and learns the rules underneath: "to interpret a game means to interpret its algorithm." [10] Compare Manovich: [games are ruled by the algorithm](/cards/galloway).
- **Dom Ford** on *Civilization V*: the game's single path of progress "reinforces a Western-centric notion of history." [11]

## For your game

An AI company buying up data centres, power and chips is playing a 4X. What is your game's map, and who is already living on it? What is on your tech tree, and does everyone climb it in the same order?
