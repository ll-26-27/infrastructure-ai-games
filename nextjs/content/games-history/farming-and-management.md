**The line in one sentence:** care pays off later. Your time and your workers are the real resources, and the game decides when you have to come back.

## How it went

In [M.U.L.E.](/gallery/mule) (Danielle Bunten Berry, 1983), settlers on another planet outfit robot workers to produce food, energy or ore and sell at live auctions. Everyone is trying to get rich, but the colony has to survive. [1] [Harvest Moon](/gallery/harvest-moon) (Yasuhiro Wada, 1996) set the farming formula: you inherit your grandfather's farm and split each day among clearing land, planting, watering, animals and neighbors. [2] Twenty years later Eric Barone, working alone for about five years, made [Stardew Valley](/gallery/stardew-valley) (2016), starting, he said, from "the basic Harvest Moon 'formula'" and expanding it "in my own way." [3] [4]

A second branch grew on social networks and phones. *Happy Farm* (5 Minutes, 2008), on a Chinese social network, let friends steal each other's crops and inspired the farm games that followed. [11] [FarmVille](/gallery/farmville) (Zynga, 2009) made crops wither if you didn't come back in time, turned friends into neighbors who help, and sold speed for real money; at its peak more than 80 million people played it each month. [5] [Hay Day](/gallery/hay-day) (2012) made the farm a supply chain of truck and boat orders, [6] and [Grow a Garden](/gallery/grow-a-garden) (2025) grows your crops while you're offline. [7] Alongside them run the management games: [Papa's Pizzeria](/gallery/papas-pizzeria)'s order line, [8] and [Papers, Please](/gallery/papers-please), which makes you the clerk at a border checkpoint whose rules change every morning. [9]

## What the mechanics argue

- **The game owns your time.** A crop that withers or a timer that calls you back is an appointment. The game decides when you play, even when you aren't playing.
- **The story and the rules can disagree** (critics call this ludonarrative dissonance). In Stardew the town hates the Joja corporation, but you can buy the town's bridge, bus and greenhouse from Joja for 135,000 gold, the same repairs you'd get by rebuilding the Community Center. [10] Both are paid for by the same money-making farm. See Community Center or Joja.
- **Work is made visible, then automated away.** M.U.L.E.'s robots, Stardew's sprinklers: the reward for managing workers well is not having to. Compare [invisible work](/cards/star). See labor allocation and automate this.
- **The farm is cozy because nobody goes hungry.** These games keep the resource loop but set it where basic needs are already met, so it feels like care, not scarcity.

## For your game

Who does the work in your game, and does the player ever see them? When does your game call the player back? Is there a commons, like Stardew's town, that someone could buy?
