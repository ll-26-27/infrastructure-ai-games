**The line in one sentence:** a city or a factory is a system of flows. Infrastructure is the whole game, and the model's assumptions are hidden in its rules.

## How it went

The line has an ancestor that was built to argue: Lizzie Magie's [The Landlord's Game](/gallery/the-landlords-game) (1904), designed to show how rent makes landlords rich, whose board became Monopoly's. [1] See the three Monopolies.

[SimCity](/gallery/simcity) (Will Wright, Maxis, 1989) began in the level editor of a helicopter game: Wright found he liked building the islands more than playing. [2] It has no way to win. You never build a house: you mark land as residential, commercial or industrial, lay power lines and roads, set taxes, and the simulated residents decide whether to move in. [2] *The Settlers* (Blue Byte, 1993) made every good travel on foot along roads, grain to the mill and flour to the bakery; in North America it was sold as *Serf City*. [3] Sid Meier's *Railroad Tycoon* (1990) made a rail network the player's company, and Chris Sawyer's *Transport Tycoon* (1994) added roads, ships and planes; Sawyer's *RollerCoaster Tycoon* (1999) made the park a business. [7] [4] *Cities: Skylines* (2015) carried SimCity's zones, roads, power and water into a modern city. [8]

[Factorio](/gallery/factorio) (Wube Software, 2016) is infrastructure with nothing else: you crash on a planet and build mines, belts, furnaces and power until you are designing a factory that builds the factory, and your pollution brings the planet's creatures down on you. [5] [Frostpunk](/gallery/frostpunk) (2018) keeps a city alive around one generator in an endless winter by passing laws, and two meters, Hope and Discontent, track what the people think. [6]

## What the mechanics argue

- **You shape a city by laying down infrastructure.** In SimCity you only zone and connect, and everything else grows out of those categories. See zoning and connected to the grid.
- **The model has opinions.** How taxes, crime or pollution work in SimCity is written into its code, and the player learns those rules as if they were how cities work. Ask what a sim leaves out.
- **Logistics is the real game.** Roads in The Settlers, belts in Factorio: these are supply chains, and they are where breakdowns happen, the moment infrastructure becomes [visible](/cards/star). Compare the breakdowns of the last two weeks.
- **Damage comes back.** Factorio's pollution and Frostpunk's Discontent make the cost of growth part of the rules, not just the story. See cascade failure and legitimacy meters.

## What critics say

Both are arguing about [simulation as argument](/cards/bogost).

- **Paul Starr**, a sociologist, asked in 1994 what SimCity's "hidden curriculum" was. "The critical problem raised by simulation is the black-box nature of the models": you can't see the assumptions, only their results. [9]
- **Ted Friedman** answered in 1999 that "any simulation will be rooted in a set of baseline assumptions," but that games, more than films or books, "reveal their own constructedness": play long enough and you learn the model's rules well enough to question them. [10]

## For your game

Infrastructure is [visible upon breakdown](/cards/star). What breaks in your game, and what does the player suddenly see when it does? Could your game make a familiar piece of infrastructure strange, so players notice it before it breaks? Which of your game's rules would someone in the course disagree with?
