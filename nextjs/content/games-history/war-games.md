**The line in one sentence:** war can be written down as rules, tables and turns, and whoever runs the rules decides what each side gets to see.

## How it went

War games began as training. In 1780, in Brunswick, Johann Christian Ludwig Hellwig published a chess variant on a board of terrain squares for young officers. Within fifty years the pieces became blocks on real maps. Venturini (1798) drew the map to scale and made the army need supplies, and Opiz (1806) brought in dice to decide casualties. In 1824 Georg von Reisswitz's *Kriegsspiel* put it all together: maps at about 1:8,000, blocks at the same scale, dice and tables built from real firing tests, turns of two minutes, written orders, and an umpire who set out only the troops each side could see. A Prussian general who watched it said, "this is no ordinary sort of game, this is schooling for war," and the king had a set sent to every regiment. [1] [2] [3]

The rules kept changing as the world did. By 1862 a new manual added railroads and the telegraph, and one historian counts at least sixteen rule sets before 1900. [1] [3] In the 1870s some officers threw out the tables and let an experienced umpire decide. [3] H. G. Wells turned it into a hobby with toy soldiers and string in *Little Wars* (1913), and ended the book against real war. [4] RAND, the Cold War think tank, went the other way: "the outcomes of moves are determined by rules, not by umpires," and one of its games already ran on an IBM computer. [5]

War games also came home. Albert Lamorisse's *La Conquête du Monde* (1957) became *Risk* (1959), armies and dice on a world map; Allan Calhamer's *Diplomacy* (sold from 1959) has no dice at all, only alliances made and broken by talking. [11] From there the line splits. Board war games with cardboard counters, combat results tables and hexagons went on sale from 1954 (*Tactics*, then *Gettysburg*). [6] [7] The umpire who tells you what happens became the Dungeon Master in *Dungeons & Dragons* (1974). [8] And the computer took over the umpire's job: Walter Bright's *Empire* (1977) blacked out the unexplored map "in a fog of war," and Sid Meier has said it inspired *Civilization*. [9] *Dune II* (Westwood, 1992) dropped turns altogether: gather, build and fight while the clock runs, the template for real-time strategy. [12] Today researchers use the same form to imagine AI futures: in *Intelligence Rising* every team commits its moves in secret and a facilitator resolves them all at once. [10]

## What the mechanics argue

- **The umpire controls the information.** Fog of war started as a person deciding what you could see (see the mechanic). In a computer game the umpire is hidden in the code, and you can't argue with it.
- **The tables hold someone's data.** Kriegsspiel's dice tables came from firing tests run between 1800 and 1812. [1] Every game's "physics" is a model somebody built, and it can be out of date. Ask the same of an AI model's training data.
- **Infrastructure enters the game when it changes what you can do.** Railroads and the telegraph were added to the rules as soon as they changed how armies moved and talked. [3] See connected to the grid.
- **Time is cut into turns.** Two-minute slices in 1824, a timed move in Wells, months per turn in AI tabletop exercises: the clock is a design choice.

## For your game

Who is the umpire in your game: a person, a rulebook, or the code? What can each player see, and what is hidden from them? If your game has a table of results, whose data would fill it?
