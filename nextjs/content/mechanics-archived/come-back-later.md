**What it is.** The game keeps running when you aren't playing. Crops ripen in real hours, shops restock overnight, a neighbor moves in on Tuesday. Come back on time and you are rewarded; come back late and things have spoiled.

## How it plays

- **FarmVille** (Zynga, 2009). Crops take real hours to grow, and if you don't harvest them in time after they ripen, they wither. Friends could visit and help tend them. [1]
- **[Stardew Valley](../gallery/stardew-valley.md)** (2016). Time runs on the game's own clock: crops need watering every day, and each season lasts 28 days. When the season changes, crops that belong to the old season die. [2]
- **Animal Crossing** (Nintendo, 2001 onward). The game follows the console's real clock and calendar. Shops close at night and holidays happen on their real dates. [3]

## What it argues

The mechanic turns the player's time into the game's resource. You don't just play; you keep appointments. The game argues that care is regular attention, and it rewards you for organizing your day around it. In free-to-play farms, that attention is also what the company sells to advertisers or converts into payments to skip the wait.

Your Oct 26 readings, "Time as Technical Medium", are about this: infrastructure doesn't just run in time, it organizes time. Eva-Maria Nyckel's study of Amazon's anticipatory shipping shows a system acting on your future before you do. Shannon Mattern's "Deep Time of Media Infrastructure" stretches the frame to geological time. A come-back-later game works in the other direction, on minutes and hours, and on you. Whose time is the game using, and who gets the value?

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Daily login rewards, energy that refills, a streak in *Duolingo*, a pet in a phone app. Pick one you have kept up for weeks. What did it make you do at a particular time? Did you ever resent it?

**Through its lineage.** *Harvest Moon* (1996 in Japan, 1997 in North America) built a farming game around seasons and daily chores. [4] *Tamagotchi* (1996) made a toy that needed you at set times. [5] Ian Bogost made *Cow Clicker* (2010) to satirize FarmVille-style games: you could click your cow once every six hours. [6]
