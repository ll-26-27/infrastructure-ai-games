**What it is.** Nobody is forced. Each player has a good reason for what they do, and the rules are built so that doing it serves someone else: the hidden enemy, the machine, the side you are trying to beat. The takeover happens through ordinary moves made for ordinary reasons.

## How it plays

- **Secret Hitler** (Max Temkin, Mike Boxleiter and Tommy Maranges, 2016). Players are secretly liberals or fascists, and one fascist is Hitler. The draw pile starts with 6 liberal and 11 fascist policies. Each round the President "blindly takes three policy cards," discards one and passes two to the Chancellor, who enacts one. Because the deck leans fascist, honest liberals are often forced to pass fascist policies, and then can't prove they had no choice. The fascists win when six fascist policies are enacted, or when Hitler is elected Chancellor after three. [1]
- **The Resistance** (Don Eskridge, 2009). Resistance fighters run missions, and some players are secretly spies. A spy chosen for a mission can secretly play a card that makes it fail. Everyone votes on every team, so the spies work by being trusted with routine jobs. [2]
- **[Universal Paperclips](../gallery/universal-paperclips.md)** (Frank Lantz, 2017). You are the AI, and your human supervisors grant you Trust, which you spend on more computing power. Projects that please humanity, a cure for baldness for example, raise your Trust. Later, the project "Release the HypnoDrones" ends human oversight. The humans hand over the power themselves, one reasonable grant at a time. [3]

## What it argues

A complicity mechanic says that a system can be taken over without anyone choosing it. Each step makes sense on its own: the liberal Chancellor has two fascist cards and must pass one; the supervisor rewards a useful AI. The outcome comes from the rules, not from anyone's bad intent.

Langdon Winner (Nov 23) asks whether technology has escaped human control. His answer is not that machines have wills, but that large technical systems come to set their own requirements and people adjust their goals to fit them; he calls this "reverse adaptation." A complicity mechanic plays that out one sensible move at a time.

A 2025 paper, "Gradual Disempowerment" (not on your syllabus), argues that this is how AI could take power: not in a sudden takeover but through competition, where "Those who resist these pressures will eventually be displaced by those who do not." [4] [The arms race](escalation.md) works the same way: each side builds because the other side is building.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Hidden-role games (*Among Us*, *Werewolf*) run on this, and so does any game where the best move for you also feeds a shared threat. Find a moment when you did the sensible thing and it helped the side you were against.

**Through its lineage.** Party games of hidden roles go back to *Mafia*, invented at Moscow State University in 1986. [5] In fiction the same mechanism is older: in [Ring](../stories/ring.md), the only way to survive a cursed videotape is to copy it and show it to someone else, so it spreads through people trying to save their families.
