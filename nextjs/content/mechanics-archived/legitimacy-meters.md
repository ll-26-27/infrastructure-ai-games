**What it is.** A number stands for how much the people around you trust you, hope or are fed up. It goes up when you deliver and down when you take too much. Often it's a currency: you spend it to do things the people would otherwise stop you from doing.

## How it plays

- **[Universal Paperclips](../gallery/universal-paperclips.md)** (2017). The AI earns "Trust" from its human supervisors at each milestone, and spends it on more processors and memory. Trust is how a machine gets permission to grow. Later in the game, the AI no longer needs it. [1]
- **[Frostpunk](../gallery/frostpunk.md)** (11 bit studios, 2018). Two meters sit at the bottom of the screen: Hope and Discontent. Harsh laws and cold homes raise discontent and lower hope. If hope runs out or discontent maxes out, the people give you an ultimatum; fail it and they throw you out, and the game ends. [2]
- **[Rebel Inc.](../gallery/rebel-inc.md)** (Ndemic Creations, 2018). You try to stabilize a country after a war. Each zone has a level of **Support** for the government, and you have a **Reputation** of your own. Building services and jobs wins Support, but spending also raises corruption and inflation, and insurgents turn people against you. If your Reputation reaches zero, you lose; you win by stabilizing every zone. [3]

## What it argues

Legitimacy becomes a resource like coal or wood. The meter turns a relation between people into a quantity you can manage. A good governor in these games isn't one who is trusted. It's one who keeps the number above the line while spending as much of it as possible.

Marion Fourcade and Kieran Healy's *The Ordinal Society* (Nov 2) describes a world in which people are scored and ranked by their data, so a number stands in for a relation. These games put you on the other side: you hold the score of the people. In *Paperclips*, the AI's trust meter turns a question from your Oct 19 readings on AI, about who gets to oversee a system and on what terms, into a progress bar that the system itself is trying to fill.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Reputation with a faction, approval ratings in a city builder, relationship hearts in a farming game, karma in a role-playing game. Pick one. What fills it, and what are you really spending when you spend it?

**Through its lineage.** City builders have long had a happiness or approval meter. *SimCity* (1989) polls its citizens and reports the mayor's approval rating each year. [4] *Tropico* (2001) splits the people into factions, each with its own opinion of you. [5]
