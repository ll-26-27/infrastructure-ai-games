**What it is.** A line of people, a stack of documents, a rulebook that changes. You compare each person's papers against the rules and decide: approve, deny, detain. You are paid for speed and penalized for mistakes.

## How it plays

- **[Papers, Please](../gallery/papers-please.md)** (Lucas Pope, 2013). You are an immigration inspector at the border of Arstotzka, a fictional Eastern-bloc country, in 1982. Each day brings new rules: new documents required, new countries barred. You're paid by the number of people you process, fined for errors, and at the end of each day your wage has to cover your family's rent, food and heat. [1]
- **The order queue**, this kit's [page on serving customers](the-order-queue.md), is the same structure with pizza instead of passports: a line, a set of requirements, a score for accuracy and speed.

## What it argues

*Papers, Please* argues through its pay structure. You can't read every story or check every detail; the wage won't allow it. So you start sorting people by the rules and nothing else, and the game lets you feel how quickly that becomes normal. The people in line become cases, and a missing stamp decides a life.

This is your Sept 21 and Sept 28 readings in a game. Nafis Hasan ("Slow Violence and Vacant Citizenship") writes about India's digital governance, where a mismatch between a person and their database record can cut them off from what they are owed. Graham Jones (Sept 28) is about categories: how we sort, and how sorting shapes what we see. Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) gives the example of race categories on the U.S. Census, a "fixed-choice category set" that took a social movement to change. The inspector's booth is where a category set meets a person.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Any game where you judge or sort: a detective game's evidence board, a *Pokémon* type chart, a moderation queue, a shop that only buys certain items. Pick one. What does the game make you stop noticing so you can sort quickly?

**Through its lineage.** Lucas Pope has said the idea came from watching immigration officers at work during his travels in Asia and trips home to the United States. [1] Games such as *Beholder* (2016), where you spy on your tenants for the state, followed with other bureaucratic jobs. [2]
