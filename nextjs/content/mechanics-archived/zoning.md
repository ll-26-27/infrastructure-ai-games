**What it is.** You don't build houses or shops yourself. You mark land with a category (residential, commercial, industrial) and the simulation fills each zone with buildings, if the conditions are right. Planning a city means painting categories and waiting.

## How it plays

- **[SimCity](../gallery/simcity.md)** (1989). Three zone types: residential, commercial and industrial. You lay them out, connect them, and the city's simulated residents decide whether to move in, set up shop or build factories. [1]
- **Cities: Skylines** (2015). The same three zones plus offices, with districts you draw on the map and give their own policies (no heavy traffic, high-rise ban, free public transport). [2]

## What it argues

Zoning games make categories into infrastructure. The player never decides what a building is; the category does. And the categories come first: they shape what the simulation can produce. A city can only grow into what the zones allow.

That is Graham Jones's point (Sept 28) carried into planning: categories don't just sort what is there, they shape what comes next. Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) notes that some infrastructure, "such as a fixed-choice category set—present[s] barriers to users that may only be changed by a full-scale social movement", and her example is the race categories on the U.S. Census. Keller Easterling (*Extrastatecraft*, Nov 9) starts from the zone, especially the free-trade zone, as one of the world's most powerful spatial technologies, a place where different rules apply. Zoning games put the zone in your hands and make it look neutral. Is it?

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Farm plots in *Stardew Valley*, rooms in *The Sims*, biomes in a survival game, any game where you designate an area for a use. Pick one. What does the designation decide for you?

**Through its lineage.** Zoning as law is about a century old: New York City passed its first comprehensive zoning resolution in 1916, and the U.S. Supreme Court upheld zoning in *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.* (1926). [3] *SimCity* turned that legal instrument into a paint tool.
