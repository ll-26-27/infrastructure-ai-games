A **mechanic** is a rule that makes something happen: a turn, a timer, a meter that fills, a worker you can move to another job. When you write about a game, it's easy to retell the story or describe the art. The mechanics are what's left: what the game lets you do, what it rewards and what it ignores. That's where a game makes its argument.

Lev Manovich, in "Database as Symbolic Form" (your Sept 21 reading), puts it this way: "While computer games do not follow a database logic, they appear to be ruled by another logic - that of the algorithm. They demand that a player executes an algorithm in order to win." The game designer and critic Ian Bogost (not on your syllabus) calls this *procedural rhetoric*: persuading through processes rather than through words or images (*Persuasive Games*, MIT Press, 2007).

So these twenty pages treat mechanics as infrastructure. Each one is a system that runs underneath the game, mostly unnoticed while it works. Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) writes that "the normally invisible quality of working infrastructure becomes visible when it breaks." Each page tries to make one of these systems visible.

## How to use a page

Each page takes about two minutes to read: what the mechanic is, how it plays in two or three games, and what it argues, read through the course's readings. Then there are two ways in:

- **From a game you know.** Pick a game you have played for a hundred hours, not one you've only heard of. You know how its parts fit together better than any summary does. Find the mechanic in it.
- **Through its lineage.** Where the mechanic came from and what it resembles. Influence in games is tangled (designers borrow from games, books, software libraries and each other), so read these as influences to trace, not a family tree.

Every page ends with something for you to fill in. Fill it in, and argue with it.
