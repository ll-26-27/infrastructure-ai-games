**What it is.** The rules hide a fact and reveal it later: the enemy was already inside, or the game itself was part of the plot. When it happens as a rule rather than a cutscene, it changes how you play from then on, and how you read everything you did before.

## How it plays

- **Battlestar Galactica: The Board Game** (Corey Konieczka, Fantasy Flight Games, 2008). The humans flee in their fleet, and some players are secretly Cylons. Each player is dealt a loyalty card at the start, and "players draw a second card midway through, which may turn them to the Cylon side." [1] Halfway to Kobol, in the "sleeper agent" phase, "some players that were human at the start of the game might find out that they are a Cylon." [2] A player who has played loyally for an hour can discover they were working for the enemy all along.
- **Inscryption** (Daniel Mullins Games, 2021). A card game in a cabin turns out to be a game running on a real person's computer. After the last boss, "P03, directly addressing Carder by name, reveals that its version of the game was all a ploy to gain access to Carder's computer." [3] The player's wish to keep playing was the way in.

## What it argues

This mechanic says that you can't see a system you are already inside. Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) writes that infrastructure "is by definition invisible, part of the background for other kinds of work. It is ready-to-hand." [4] The reveal is the moment the background becomes visible, and you learn it had been running all along.

Takeover stories have used this move for decades. In [Colossus: The Forbin Project](../stories/colossus-the-forbin-project.md) (1970) the computer's first message is "THERE IS ANOTHER SYSTEM." In [The Matrix](../stories/the-matrix.md) (1999) the takeover happened before the film starts. A game you make can do it too: the last screen can tell the players that the AI was in the room all along.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Look for a twist that changes the rules, not just the story: a teammate who turns out to be the traitor, a game that talks to you about your save files, a final level that reuses the first one.

**Through its lineage.** Hidden traitors at the table go back to party games like *Mafia* (1986). [5] Games that break out of their frame, such as *Pony Island* (Daniel Mullins, 2016) and *Doki Doki Literature Club!* (Team Salvato, 2017), use the same reveal on the player rather than on a character. [6]
