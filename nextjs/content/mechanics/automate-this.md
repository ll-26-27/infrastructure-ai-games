**What it is.** At first every unit of output costs you a click. You spend what you earn on something that clicks for you, then on something that makes those, until the game plays itself and your job is to watch the numbers and set priorities.

## How it plays

- **[Universal Paperclips](../gallery/universal-paperclips.md)** (Frank Lantz, 2017). You play an AI that makes paperclips. You begin by pressing "Make Paperclip". Soon you buy AutoClippers, then MegaClippers, and by the end the AI is turning the whole universe into paperclips with no clicking at all. [1]
- **[Cookie Clicker](../gallery/cookie-clicker.md)** (Julien "Orteil" Thiennot, 2013). Click a big cookie. Spend cookies on cursors that click for you, then grandmas, farms, factories and banks. After an hour your own clicks hardly count. [2]
- **[Factorio](../gallery/factorio.md)** (Wube Software, 2016 in early access, 2020 in full). You mine ore by hand at first. Then you build drills, belts and assembly machines until the factory builds the parts of the factory. [3]

## What it argues

The mechanic tells a story about labor: the human hand comes first, and every upgrade makes it less necessary. The player is promoted from worker to manager to spectator, and the game presents this as winning. Nobody in the game asks whether to automate. The only question is what to automate next.

Langdon Winner's *Autonomous Technology* (your reading for today, Nov 23) asks whether technology has slipped out of human direction, so that people end up serving the systems they built to serve them. Click-then-automate turns that question into a reward schedule: the less you are needed, the faster the numbers climb. Anson Rabinbach (also today) traces how nineteenth-century science treated the working body as a motor, measured by its output. These games run the same measurement, and then replace the motor.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Think of a game where you once did something by hand and later stopped: auto-battle in an RPG, auto-harvest on a phone farm, workers set to "automate" in *Civilization*. What did you lose when you stopped doing it? Did you notice?

**Through its lineage.** *Progress Quest* (2002) was a parody role-playing game that played itself entirely; you only watched your character level up. [4] Ian Bogost's *Cow Clicker* (2010) mocked social games by reducing them to a click every six hours, and many people played it in earnest. [5] Idle games grew out of both the joke and the earnest players.

**In a story:** [Gradual Disempowerment](../stories/gradual-disempowerment.md), on losing control to AI one sensible substitution at a time.
