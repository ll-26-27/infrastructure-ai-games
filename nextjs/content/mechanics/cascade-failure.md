**What it is.** Things are linked, so a failure doesn't stay where it started. One overload causes the next, and a small problem becomes a chain reaction faster than you can respond. The game is about keeping enough slack in the system that the first failure doesn't set off the rest.

## How it plays

- **[Pandemic](../gallery/pandemic.md)** (2008). A city can hold three disease cubes. If it has to take a fourth, it has an *outbreak*: every city connected to it gets a cube instead. If one of those already has three, it breaks out too, and the chain keeps going. Eight outbreaks and the players lose. Epidemic cards make it worse: they raise the infection rate and put the cities already infected back on top of the deck, so the same hot spots come back. [1]
- **[They Are Billions](../gallery/they-are-billions.md)** (2017 in early access, 2019 in full). If the infected break through your walls, the buildings and colonists they reach become infected and spread the infection further inside. One gap in the wall can lose a colony that took hours to build. [2]

## What it argues

These games teach you that a system's danger is in its links, not its parts. Every city in *Pandemic* is fine on its own until its neighbors are full. The lesson the game drills into you is to look at what is connected to what, and keep slack where things are tightly coupled.

This is Charles Perrow's argument in "Complexity, Coupling, and Catastrophe" (*Normal Accidents*, Nov 16): when a system is both complex (parts interact in unexpected ways) and tightly coupled (one part's failure passes quickly to the next), accidents are "normal". They come from the system's design, not from a careless operator. Michael Fisch's "Ninety Seconds" (also Nov 16) studies Tokyo's commuter trains, which run on margins measured in seconds, so a delay at one point travels down the line. And Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) reminds us that infrastructure "becomes visible upon breakdown." A cascade is how a breakdown makes a whole system visible at once.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Chain reactions turn up in puzzle games (*Candy Crush*, *Tetris* combos), in survival games (a fire spreading through a base), in city builders (a power failure darkening a district). Pick one. Is the cascade your reward or your punishment?

**Through its lineage.** Dominoes and *Jenga* are cascades you can hold. In the real world, large blackouts are a textbook case: the 2003 Northeast blackout began in Ohio, where power lines sagged into overgrown trees and tripped while a software bug silenced the control room's alarms, and their load shifted onto other lines until those failed too.
