**What it is.** One game, two routes to the same repairs. Fill in a collection, item by item, and the town is fixed by and for the community. Or buy a membership and pay cash, and a company fixes it and takes over the building.

## How it plays

- **[Stardew Valley](../gallery/stardew-valley.md)** (Eric Barone, 2016). The town of Pelican Town has a run-down Community Center. Its rooms hold "bundles", lists of things to bring: spring crops, fish from the lake, minerals from the mine. Finishing a room repairs part of the town: the bridge to the quarry, the minecarts, the greenhouse, the bus to the desert. [1]
- **The other route.** At the JojaMart, the big store that is putting the town's general store out of business, you can buy a Joja membership. Then each repair is bought outright with gold through the "Joja Community Development Form", and the Community Center becomes a Joja warehouse. [2]
- **[Harvest Moon](../gallery/harvest-moon.md)** (1996), the farming game *Stardew Valley*'s creator has named as its inspiration, also asks you to restore a farm; *Stardew* adds the choice of who restores the town. [3]

## What it argues

Both routes give you the same bridge and the same bus. The game argues through what it costs. The Community Center route costs time, attention and a full year of fishing, farming and digging. The Joja route costs money and nothing else, and it's faster. The game doesn't stop you choosing Joja. It just makes you feel what goes away when you do: the building, the town's own reasons to gather in it, the story the bundles were telling.

The bundles are also a database. Each is a category ("Spring Foraging", "River Fish") with slots to fill, and the whole catalog is a picture of what the valley is. Graham Jones's "Categorize, Recategorize, Repeat" (Sept 28) is about how the categories we file things in shape what we notice next. Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) writes: "Study a city and neglect its sewers and power supplies (as many have), and you miss essential aspects of distributional justice and planning power." *Stardew* puts the question of who provides the town's infrastructure at the center of the game, and then lets you answer it.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Many games offer a paid shortcut to something you could earn: buying instead of crafting, a premium pass instead of grinding. Pick one you know well. What does the slow route give you that the shortcut doesn't?

**Through its lineage.** Collecting a full set is an old pleasure in games: the *Pokémon* Pokédex (1996) and *Animal Crossing*'s museum both turn the world into a catalog to complete. [4] *Stardew* attaches the catalog to public works.
