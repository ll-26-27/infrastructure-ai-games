**What it is.** Placing a building isn't enough. It has to be joined to the networks that make it work, and the game shows you, in red icons and dark windows, everything that isn't connected yet.

## How it plays

- **[SimCity](../gallery/simcity.md)** (Will Wright, Maxis, 1989). Zones don't develop until they are connected to a power plant, by power lines or through neighboring powered zones. An unpowered zone shows a flashing lightning-bolt symbol. [1]
- **Cities: Skylines** (Colossal Order, Paradox Interactive, 2015). Every building needs electricity, water and sewage, carried by overhead power lines and underground pipes. Cut a building off and it can end up abandoned. [2]
- **[Factorio](../gallery/factorio.md)** (2016 onward). Machines need power from poles and materials from belts. An assembler with nothing on its belt just stops, and the stoppage travels down the line. [3]

## What it argues

These games teach you to see a city as its networks. The buildings are what you notice; the power lines, pipes and belts are what makes them work. After a few hours of *SimCity*, you start noticing the substations and manholes on your walk to class.

Your course description asks: "What happens when we see air as media, cities as computers, screens as culture, or race as infrastructure?" Grid games are a way of seeing cities as computers. Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) lists infrastructure's properties, among them that it is "built on an installed base": "Optical fibers run along old railroad lines; new systems are designed for backward compatibility." In most of these games you lay the grid on empty land. Ask what the games would be like if every new line had to follow an old one.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Redstone in *Minecraft*, a base's power in a survival game, the supply lines in a strategy game, the Wi-Fi bars on your own phone. Pick one. What happens to things that aren't connected?

**Through its lineage.** *SimCity* grew out of the level editor Will Wright built for an earlier game, *Raid on Bungeling Bay* (1984), and Wright drew on the system dynamics of Jay Forrester, whose *Urban Dynamics* (1969) modeled a city on a computer. [1] [4] Manovich (Sept 21) quotes Wright too: "Playing the game is a continuous loop between the user (viewing the outcomes and inputting decisions) and the computer (calculating outcomes and displaying them back to the user). The user is trying to build a mental model of the computer model."
