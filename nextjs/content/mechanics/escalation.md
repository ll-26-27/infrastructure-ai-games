**What it is.** Two sides compete, and every gain by one forces the other to answer. Neither side can stop alone without falling behind. Often there is a shared track of danger that both sides push, and whoever pushes it over the edge loses.

## How it plays

- **Twilight Struggle** (Ananda Gupta and Jason Matthews, GMT Games, 2005). A two-player board game of the Cold War. A DEFCON track starts at 5. Coups and some events push it down, and if a player's action brings it to 1, nuclear war breaks out and that player loses. Each turn, though, each side must also carry out a required number of military operations or give the other side points. So both players have to keep the danger high without being the one who tips it. [1]
- **DEFCON** (Introversion Software, 2006). A strategy game of global nuclear war, sold with the tagline "Everybody dies." The DEFCON level drops on a fixed timer, unlocking more aggressive moves as it falls, and you score by how many of the other side's people die compared with your own. [2]
- **[Civilization](../gallery/civilization.md)** (1991 onward). Once rivals can build nuclear weapons, each civilization's arsenal gives the others a reason to build their own. [3]

## What it argues

An arms race is a mechanic in which nobody chooses the outcome. Each move is reasonable, given the other side's last move, and the sum is a catastrophe neither side wanted. *Twilight Struggle* makes the point with its rules: the game makes you take risks, then punishes whoever took the last one.

Charles Perrow's *Normal Accidents* (Nov 16) argues that in tightly coupled, complex systems, accidents are "normal": they follow from how the system is built, not from one person's error. An arms race is a coupling machine. Ask also who gains while the race goes on. In *DEFCON* nobody wins, but in a real arms race the suppliers to both sides do well. Imagine attackers and defenders who both buy their help from the same AI: the race itself is the AI's business.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Competitive multiplayer games often have one: two players upgrading in turn, a ranked season where everyone grinds because everyone else is grinding, an escalation in a strategy game neither of you can stop. Where did it come from? Could you have stopped?

**Through its lineage.** The mathematician Lewis Fry Richardson modeled arms races as equations in which each country's armament grows with the other's (*Arms and Insecurity*, 1960). [4] *DEFCON*'s designers drew on the 1983 film *WarGames*, which ends on a computer concluding that nuclear war is a game in which "the only winning move is not to play." [2]

**In a story:** in [Colossus](../stories/colossus-novel.md), a defense computer built for the arms race takes over with the missiles people already built.
