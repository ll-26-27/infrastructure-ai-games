**What it is.** The whole map exists from the start, but you can see only what your units can see. Places you have never visited are black. Places you have visited but left are shown as they were when you last looked. Knowing the map is something you have to earn.

## How it plays

- **[Civilization](../gallery/civilization.md)** (1991 onward). The map starts hidden, and units lift the fog as they explore, revealing coastlines, resources and neighbors. [1]
- **StarCraft** (Blizzard, 1998). Unexplored ground is black. Ground you have seen but no longer watch turns gray and shows only what was there when you left, so the enemy can build behind it unseen. [2]
- **[XCOM](../gallery/xcom.md)** (1994; remade 2012). On each mission you see only what your soldiers can see, and aliens appear from the dark. Every step forward is a risk. [3]

## What it argues

Fog of war teaches you to think of knowledge as territory: what you know is where you've been, and the unknown is waiting to be revealed. When the fog lifts, the map is already there, complete. It was never in question, only hidden from you. That's a claim about the world as a database whose records you unlock one tile at a time.

Lev Manovich (Sept 21) argues that a database presents the world as a list of items with no story attached. A fog-of-war map is a database with a reveal animation. Susan Leigh Star (Sept 21) notes that one person's infrastructure is another's obstacle: "For the person in a wheelchair, the stairs and doorjamb in front of a building are not seamless subtenders of use, but barriers." Fog shows the same relativity on screen: the map looks different depending on whose units are looking. Who in the world you study sees the whole map, and who only sees the fog?

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Open-world games, survival games, roguelikes and battle royales all hide parts of the map. Pick one you know well. Does the fog come back once you leave? What do you do differently because you can't see?

**Through its lineage.** Officers in the nineteenth century trained with *Kriegsspiel*, a war game in which an umpire told each side only what its troops could see. [4] The phrase "fog of war" is usually traced to Clausewitz's *On War*, which compares war's uncertainty to fog, though he didn't use that exact phrase. [5]
