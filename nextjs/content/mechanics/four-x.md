**What it is.** You start with one settlement on a mostly dark map. You send scouts out (explore), found new cities (expand), put the land to work (exploit) and fight whoever is in the way (exterminate). Each turn the loop runs again, a little bigger.

## How it plays

- **[Civilization](../gallery/civilization.md)** (Sid Meier, MicroProse, 1991 onward). You lead one people from a single settler to the space age. Cities work the land around them for food, production and gold, borders grow, and other civilizations become trading partners or targets. [1]
- **Master of Orion** (Simtex, MicroProse, 1993). The same loop across a galaxy of star systems. A preview of this game in *Computer Gaming World* (1993), by Alan Emrich, gave the genre its name: "eXplore, eXpand, eXploit and eXterminate." [2]
- **[Unciv](../gallery/unciv.md)** and **[Freeciv](../gallery/freeciv.md)**. Free, open-source versions of the Civilization loop made by volunteers. You can read their code, so you can see exactly how the loop is written. [3] [4]

## What it argues

The 4X loop is a theory of history: a people starts small, the world is land waiting to be claimed, and greatness is measured by how much of the map you hold and how much the land produces. The four X's are verbs, and every one of them is something done *to* the map.

Keller Easterling (*Extrastatecraft*, Nov 9) writes about "infrastructure space": roads, zones and standards that shape what can happen in a place, often with more force than the law. In a 4X game, infrastructure space is all there is: roads, cities, borders and improvements, laid down one turn at a time. Your Sept 2 reading by Sophie Toupin appeared in an e-flux series on the coloniality of infrastructure. Ask that question of the 4X map: who was there before your first city, and how does the game show them?

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Plenty of games run a smaller 4X loop: a base you grow in a survival game, a village in *Clash of Clans*, a colony in *Minecraft*. Pick one. Which of the four X's does it actually use, and which does it leave out?

**Through its lineage.** Walter Bright's *Empire* (1977), a wargame for mainframes, already had a hidden map, cities that produce units, and conquest. [5] Francis Tresham's board game *Civilization* (1980) lent its name to Sid Meier's game. [1] These are influences to trace, not a single line of descent.
