**What it is.** The settlement has a population, the buildings have open jobs, and there are never enough hands. You move people from the mine to the kitchen to the hospital, and every move leaves something else short.

## How it plays

- **[Frostpunk](../gallery/frostpunk.md)** (2018). Your citizens are workers, engineers and, if you pass the law that allows it, children. You assign them to coal mines, cookhouses, workshops and infirmaries, and anyone unassigned shows up as idle. [1]
- **[Civilization](../gallery/civilization.md)** (1991 onward). Each citizen of a city works one tile of land, or works in the city as a specialist. Moving a citizen from a farm to a mine trades food for production. [2]
- **Agricola** (Uwe Rosenberg, 2007). A board game of seventeenth-century farming. Each round you place your family members on action spaces (plow a field, take grain, build a room), and a space one player takes is closed to the others. Children mean more workers later, and more mouths to feed now. [3]

## What it argues

These games present people as labor, available to be moved. The player is the planner who sees every job and every worker at once, and assigning them never costs anything but the output you lose. Nobody in *Frostpunk* refuses a job. The pressure comes from the laws you pass, through the Hope and Discontent meters, not from the people.

Anson Rabinbach (Nov 23) traces the nineteenth-century science of work that saw the human body as a motor, a store of energy to be spent efficiently. Sandro Mezzadra and Brett Neilson ("Operations of Platforms", Nov 9) describe how digital platforms now do the allocating: an app assigns the delivery, the ride, the task. In a game, you are the platform. What would it feel like to play as one of the workers being moved?

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Base builders, colony sims, *The Sims*, a guild in an MMO, even a sports team's lineup. Pick one. Who are the workers, who decides where they go, and can they say no?

**Through its lineage.** Board game designers usually point to *Caylus* (2005) as the game that made "worker placement" a genre, with *Agricola* close behind. [3] [4] Before that, M.U.L.E. (1983) had players install a robot on each plot of land to farm, mine or make energy. [5]
