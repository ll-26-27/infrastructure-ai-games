**What it is.** Laws and policies sit in a menu next to buildings and research. Each one gives you something (more output, more order, more hope) and costs something (discontent, freedom, money). You unlock them, slot them in and swap them out.

## How it plays

- **[Frostpunk](../gallery/frostpunk.md)** (2018). The Book of Laws lets you sign laws such as Child Labour – Safe Jobs (children work in places like the cookhouse) and Extended Shift (14-hour workdays). Each signed law changes your workforce, your meters and the people's mood, and once signed, a law can't be unsigned. [1]
- **Civilization VI** (Firaxis, 2016). Your government has slots for policy cards (military, economic, diplomatic, wildcard). You swap cards in as your needs change, the way you'd swap equipment. [2]
- **Tropico** (2001 onward). As a Caribbean dictator, you issue edicts, from literacy programs to martial law, each pleasing some factions and angering others. [3]

## What it argues

Laws-as-upgrades makes governance look like optimization. A law is a modifier: +15% coal, −10 hope. It's legible, reversible in some games, and judged only by its effects on the meters. *Frostpunk* is unusual because it makes you feel the cost. The child labor law is efficient, and the game wants you to notice that you signed it.

Kaspar Villadsen (Oct 5) reads Foucault's idea of technology broadly: techniques and procedures for governing conduct, not only machines. Laws in these games are exactly that, a toolkit for steering a population. Nafis Hasan (Sept 21) shows digital governance in India working through databases and records, where a technical setting can decide who counts as a citizen. Keller Easterling (Nov 9) argues that infrastructure often governs more than law does. Which upgrade menus in your own life act as law without being called law?

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Perks, policies, house rules, server settings, difficulty options. Pick one you know well. Is there a choice everyone makes because it's "optimal"? What does that tell you about what the game values?

**Through its lineage.** Positech's *Democracy* (2005) built a whole game out of policies: a web of sliders connected to voter groups and outcomes. [4] Civilization's governments go back to the 1991 game, where choosing one changed how the whole empire ran. [5]
