**What it is.** A chart of every technology in the game, joined by arrows. You research one at a time, and each one opens the ones that depend on it. Writing before philosophy, gunpowder before rifling, computers before the internet. To win, you climb.

## How it plays

- **[Civilization](../gallery/civilization.md)** (Sid Meier, MicroProse, 1991 onward). Researching one technology opens the ones that depend on it, from pottery, the wheel and the alphabet to spaceflight. One way to win is to research far enough to launch a ship to another star. *Civilization VI* (2016) adds a second tree, for civics such as political philosophy. [1]
- **Age of Empires II** (Ensemble Studios, 1999). Your civilization advances through ages (Dark, Feudal, Castle, Imperial). Each age opens new buildings and units, and you must pay to advance. [2]
- **[Universal Paperclips](../gallery/universal-paperclips.md)** (2017). "Projects" appear once you have met their conditions, and each opens the next: from better wire buying to the AI's first steps beyond Earth. [3]

## What it argues

The tech tree is a theory of history drawn as a chart. Technology comes in a fixed order, every society climbs the same tree, and being further up is being more advanced. Two civilizations that start on opposite sides of the map end up researching the same things.

Your readings argue the opposite. Amit Prasad (*Imperial Technoscience*, Sept 14) shows that MRI was not invented in one place and then spread; its history ran between the US, Britain and India at once. Yuk Hui ("Cosmotechnics as Cosmopolitics", Oct 5) argues that there is no single universal technology. Different cultures join technical activity to different understandings of the cosmos and of what is right. A tech tree has room for one cosmotechnics, and it's an infrastructure for imagining progress: it decides what can be researched and what comes first, and the player rarely notices.

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Skill trees, crafting recipes and upgrade paths are tech trees too. Pick one you know by heart. Is there a branch everyone takes? What would the game be like if the order were shuffled?

**Through its lineage.** Francis Tresham's board game *Civilization* (1980) had "civilization advances" that unlocked one another. Sid Meier's 1991 game borrowed that name, and its tree took a similar shape. [1] [4] Read it as one influence among several, not as the origin.
