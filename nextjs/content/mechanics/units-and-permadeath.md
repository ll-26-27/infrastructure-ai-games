**What it is.** In war games people are *units*: a type (archer, rifleman, medic), a health number, a few skills. Most games let you build more of them. Some give each unit a name and a history, and then make its death permanent, so losing one costs you something you can't buy back.

## How it plays

- **[Civilization](../gallery/civilization.md)** (1991 onward). Units are types: Warriors, Settlers, Workers. When one dies you build another just like it. [1]
- **[XCOM](../gallery/xcom.md)** (*UFO Defense*, 1994; *Enemy Unknown*, Firaxis, 2012). Each soldier has a name, a nationality, a rank and abilities earned on missions, and you can rename and dress them. Dead soldiers stay dead, and the 2012 game lists them on a memorial in your base. [2]
- **Fire Emblem** (Intelligent Systems, 1990 onward). Each unit is a character with a story. In the series' classic mode, a unit that falls in battle is gone for the rest of the game. [3]

## What it argues

The unit is a category. It turns a person into a token of a type, defined by what it can do on the map: move three tiles, attack at range, heal. Graham Jones (Sept 28) describes analysis as identifying "particular instances of some behavior as tokens of types." War games do this to people all the time.

Permadeath pushes back. *XCOM* builds attachment by giving each soldier a name and a past, then makes the loss stick. Anson Rabinbach (Nov 23) traces how the science of work came to treat the body as a motor, measured by output. Units are motors: stats, upkeep, replacement cost. Permadeath is where the game admits they were more than that, and it uses the admission to make you play more carefully. Is that a critique of the category, or a better way of managing it?

## Two ways in

**From a game you know.** Think of a game where you've lost a character you cared about: a party member, a pet, a save file. Did the game make the loss permanent? How did that change the way you played after?

**Through its lineage.** Tabletop war games moved cardboard counters with printed numbers long before computers. [4] Roguelikes, from *Rogue* (1980) on, made death permanent for the player's own character. [5] *XCOM* applied permadeath to a whole squad.
