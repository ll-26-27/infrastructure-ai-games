**The idea in one line:** a game is what it makes you do. Its mechanics, the actions you repeat, what the game counts, and what it rewards and punishes, are where it makes its argument.

## The plot-summary trap

Ask someone about a film and they will usually tell you the plot. Essays about film often do the same, and reduce the film to its script: the shots and the editing get left out, or get named ("a close-up") without being read. With poems, the critic Cleanth Brooks called this "the heresy of paraphrase" (*The Well Wrought Urn*, 1947).

Games have their own version. Story and look are easy to talk about, which is why the reviews of *Grand Theft Auto VI* call it a love story and an epic (see [Why games?](/why)). The game as a game is in its mechanics. Jesse Schell names four elements every game is made of: story, aesthetics, mechanics and technology ([the tetrad](/reading-mechanics/tetrad)). The first two are home ground for anyone trained on books and films. Mechanics are the new move.

## Form makes the argument

This is an old idea, and it is already in your readings for this course.

- **Lev Manovich**, "Database as Symbolic Form" (1999; on the syllabus for September 21), borrows his title from Erwin Panofsky's *Perspective as Symbolic Form* (1927), who took "symbolic form" from Ernst Cassirer. Each time the claim is that a form carries a way of seeing the world. Manovich on games: "They demand that a player executes an algorithm in order to win." As you play, you learn "its hidden logic, in short its algorithm."
- **Susan Leigh Star** (1999; also September 21): "The normally invisible quality of working infrastructure becomes visible when it breaks" (p. 382). Mechanics are a game's infrastructure: you stop seeing them after a few hours, and see them again when they glitch or feel unfair.
- **Langdon Winner**, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980): technical arrangements settle political questions by how they are built. His *Autonomous Technology* (1977) is on the syllabus for November 23. A game rule is an artifact in his sense.
- **Bruno Latour**, "Technology Is Society Made Durable" (1991), and **Raymond Williams** on "structures of feeling" (*Marxism and Literature*, 1977): a rule holds a social arrangement in place, and it carries a feeling, the anxiety of a timer or the comfort of a harvest that comes back each season.
- **Ian Bogost** brings the move to games as procedural rhetoric, "the art of persuasion through rule-based representations and interactions rather than the spoken word, writing, images, or moving pictures" (*Persuasive Games*, 2007, preface).

The literary version is **Viktor Shklovsky**, "Art as Device" (1917): habit makes us stop perceiving things, and art exists "to make the stone stony" (Lemon and Reis's translation, 1965). A game can do that to a system you live inside every day.

## Try it

1. **Pick a game you have played a lot**, on a phone, a console or a board.
2. **Write its plot in one sentence.** (If it has none, say so.)
3. **List three mechanics.** What do you do over and over? What does the game count? What does it reward, and what does it punish?
4. **Say what one mechanic argues:** "By making me ____, the game argues that ____."
5. **Find the reading that would recognize it:** Star, Manovich, Perrow, Winner, someone else?

Compare with someone next to you: your plots will differ, so check whether your mechanics overlap. Then look your mechanic up in the [mechanics pages](/mechanics), find other games that use it in the [gallery](/gallery), and find a novel or film that tells the same story in [the stories](/stories). If something isn't there, add it: one mechanic, one game, one sentence on what it argues.
