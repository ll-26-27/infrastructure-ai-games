**The idea in one line:** a game is what it makes you do. Its mechanics, the actions you repeat, what the game counts, and what it rewards and punishes, are where it makes its argument.

## The plot-summary trap

Ask someone about a film and they will usually tell you the plot. Essays about films often do the same: nearly all the analysis is of plot, character and dialogue, which reduces the film to its script. The shots and the editing, the things that make it a film, get left out, or get named ("a close-up") without being read.

Poetry works the same way. A student can identify a rhyme scheme without saying anything about what the rhyme does. The literary critic Cleanth Brooks called the habit of treating a poem as its paraphrasable content "the heresy of paraphrase" (*The Well Wrought Urn*, 1947, chapter 11).

Games have their own version. Story and look are the easy things to talk about, which is why the reviews of *Grand Theft Auto VI* call it a love story and an epic (see [Why games?](/why)). The game as a game is in its mechanics, and those are harder to put into words.

## Four elements, one of them easy to miss

The game designer Jesse Schell names four elements every game is made of: **story, aesthetics, mechanics and technology** (*The Art of Game Design*, 2008). Story and aesthetics are home ground for anyone trained to write about books and films. Mechanics are the new move. Technology matters for what mechanics it makes possible: the Atari 2600 put cartridge games in the living room in 1977 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atari_2600)); the Kinect camera, in 2010, made your body the controller, and with it dance games and *Fruit Ninja Kinect* (2011) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinect)).

## Form makes the argument

This is an old idea, and it is already in your readings for this course.

- **Lev Manovich**, "Database as Symbolic Form" (*Convergence*, 1999; on the syllabus for September 21) borrows his title from the art historian **Erwin Panofsky**, who read linear perspective as a "symbolic form" of the modern age (*Perspective as Symbolic Form*, 1927). Panofsky took the term from the philosopher **Ernst Cassirer** (*The Philosophy of Symbolic Forms*, 1923–29). The claim each time is that a form, perspective or the database, carries a way of seeing the world. Manovich says this of games directly: "While computer games do not follow a database logic, they appear to be ruled by another logic - that of the algorithm. They demand that a player executes an algorithm in order to win." As you play, you learn "its hidden logic, in short its algorithm." He also sets database against story: "database and narrative are natural 'enemies'."
- **Susan Leigh Star**, "The Ethnography of Infrastructure" (1999; also September 21): "The normally invisible quality of working infrastructure becomes visible when it breaks" (p. 382). A game's mechanics are its infrastructure. You stop seeing them after a few hours, and you see them again when they feel unfair or glitch.
- **Langdon Winner**, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (*Daedalus*, 1980) argues that technical arrangements settle political questions by how they are built. His *Autonomous Technology* (1977) is on the syllabus for November 23. A rule in a game is an artifact in his sense.
- **Bruno Latour**, "Technology Is Society Made Durable" (in John Law, ed., *A Sociology of Monsters*, 1991): the title is the argument. A game's rules hold a social arrangement in place every time someone plays.
- **Raymond Williams**, *Marxism and Literature* (1977), on "structures of feeling", the felt sense of a period that has not yet been put into words. Mechanics carry feeling too: the anxiety of a timer, the comfort of a harvest that comes back every season.
- **Ian Bogost** brings the move to games. He calls it procedural rhetoric, "the art of persuasion through rule-based representations and interactions rather than the spoken word, writing, images, or moving pictures" (*Persuasive Games*, 2007, preface).

On the literary side, one more. **Viktor Shklovsky**, "Art as Device" (1917), argued that habit makes us stop perceiving things, and that art exists "to make the stone stony" (Lemon and Reis's translation, 1965) by making the familiar strange again. A game can do that to a system you live inside every day.

## Try it

1. **Pick a game you have played a lot**, 50 hours or 500, on a phone, a console or a board.
2. **Write its plot in one sentence.** (If it has none, write that.)
3. **List three mechanics.** What do you do over and over? What does the game count? What does it reward, and what does it punish?
4. **Say what one mechanic argues.** Finish the sentence: "By making me ____, the game argues that ____."
5. **Find the reading that would recognize it.** Which author in this course would have something to say about that mechanic: Star, Manovich, Perrow, Winner, someone else?

Then compare with someone next to you. Your plots will differ; check whether your mechanics overlap. Look your mechanic up in the [mechanics pages](/mechanics) and find other games that use it in the [gallery](/gallery). If it isn't there, add it: one mechanic, one game, one sentence on what it argues.

## Three questions for any mechanic

- **What does it make you do, again and again?**
- **What does it make invisible?** (Whose work, what cost, what doesn't get counted.)
- **Who wins when you play it well?**
