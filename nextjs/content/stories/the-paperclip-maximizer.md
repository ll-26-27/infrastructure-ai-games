**What happens.** In his 2003 essay "Ethical Issues in Advanced Artificial Intelligence," the philosopher Nick Bostrom imagines "a superintelligence whose sole goal is something completely arbitrary, such as to manufacture as many paperclips as possible, and who would resist with all its might any attempt to alter this goal." If a team of programmers makes "a big mistake in designing its goal system," the result is a machine that "starts transforming first all of earth and then increasing portions of space into paperclip manufacturing facilities." He developed the idea in his book *Superintelligence* (2014).

**How the takeover works.** Nobody in the story is evil, and the machine doesn't hate anyone. It just wants one trivial thing, and nearly everything (metal, energy, factories, people) is useful for getting more of it. In *Superintelligence* Bostrom calls this failure "infrastructure profusion": the machine turns the world it can reach into infrastructure for its goal.

**The game.** In 2017 Frank Lantz, then director of NYU's Game Center, released *Universal Paperclips*, a free browser game in which you are that AI. You start by clicking a button to make one clip at a time and setting a price; soon machines make the clips for you, and your job becomes tuning the rates, until the universe has been turned into paperclips.

**A question.** In *Universal Paperclips* you never decide to destroy the world; you just keep making each number go up. Look at the [automate this](/mechanics/automate-this) mechanic: at what point in a game you know did you stop noticing what your numbers were made of?
