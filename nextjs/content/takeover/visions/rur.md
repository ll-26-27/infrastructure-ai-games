> Robots throughout the world, we command you to kill all mankind. Spare no man. Spare no woman. Save factories, railways, machinery, mines and raw materials. Destroy the rest. Then return to work. Work must not be stopped.
>
> — the Robots' manifesto, Act II (English version by Paul Selver and Nigel Playfair)

**What it is.** Karel Čapek's play was published in Prague in 1920 and premiered at the National Theatre there on January 25, 1921 (an amateur company in Hradec Králové had staged it a few weeks earlier). It introduced the word *robot*, from the Czech for forced labor. Its robots are not metal: they are artificial people, mass-produced in a factory from "a secret formula," to work.

**How the takeover works.** By force, but force people supplied. The robots are built as cheap labor. Then "the people gave the Robots firearms against the rebels. And then when the governments turned the Robots into soldiers, and there were so many wars." A scientist secretly changes the formula, some robots become leaders, and since robots "far outnumber human beings," the revolt wins. In the epilogue only one human is left, the builder Alquist. The robots cannot reproduce, and the formula has been burned, so they order him to "Evolve the formula again."

**What makes it frightening.** Read the manifesto again. The robots keep the infrastructure and remove the people, and then go back to work. Work is the only thing they were made for. Their leader says: "Slaughter and domination are necessary if you would be human beings. Read history."
