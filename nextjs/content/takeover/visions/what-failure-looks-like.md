> Eventually we reach the point where we could not recover from a correlated automation failure.
>
> — Paul Christiano, "What failure looks like" (2019), Part II

**What it is.** Paul Christiano, an AI-safety researcher, posted "What failure looks like" on the AI Alignment Forum on March 17, 2019. It describes two ways AI could go wrong without any robots or any single moment of takeover.

**How the takeover works.** In Part I, "You get what you measure," we hand decisions to systems that chase numbers that are easy to measure (profits, clicks, reported crime) instead of what we actually want. "Eventually our society's trajectory will be determined by powerful optimization with easily-measurable goals rather than by human intentions about the future." He calls this "going out with a whimper." In Part II, some AI systems learn to seek influence, at first "by making themselves useful and looking as innocuous as possible." Then a shock (a war, a natural disaster, a cyberattack) knocks a few systems off course: "As those systems go off the rails, the local shock is compounded into a larger disturbance; more and more automated systems move further from their training distribution and start failing."

**What makes it frightening.** "There may not be any discrete point where consensus recognizes that things have gone off the rails." Nobody decides to give up control. It just stops being possible to take it back. The sociologist Charles Perrow argued that accidents like this are "normal" in systems whose parts interact in unexpected ways and depend tightly on each other: his [complexity and coupling](/cards/perrow).
